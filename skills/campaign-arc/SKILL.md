# Campaign Arc

A campaign arc has a theme and a resolution, and six beats between them. The beats are consequences, not events — each one is defined by what must be *different* after it lands, not by how it lands.

## Before you plan — ask for what matters

Ask only for what is missing. Defaults in brackets.

1. **Premise** — one sentence: what is this campaign about? [ask]
2. **Party level range** [1–10, or the range the user gives]
3. **Expected length** — sessions, roughly [10–15]
4. **Tone** [eerie-first with dry humour]
5. **What the DM already knows** — a villain, a location, a faction, a fact the party already learned [none]

## The rules

- **Beats are consequences, not events.** An event is one specific thing happening ("the guildmaster signs the decree"). A consequence is a changed state of the world ("the party loses the one ally inside the guild who could vouch for them"). A dozen scenes could deliver a consequence; a single pre-empted event kills an event-shaped beat. Write every beat as a before/after.
- **Theme and resolution first.** Theme: one sentence — what the story is ultimately *about*, not the threat, its meaning. Resolution: the committed endpoint shape — if the party succeeds, what is the emotional truth? Commit to the shape, keep specific events open.
- **The Midpoint Shift is non-negotiable.** Without it, act 2 drifts indefinitely. If it has not landed by about halfway through the expected session count, escalate world pressure until it does.
- **All Is Lost is earned, not punitive.** It comes from the world's logic, not arbitrary bad luck. The party should feel it coming and be unable to stop it.
- **Apply world pressure before each beat.** Each beat has a built-in faction or NPC move that creates the conditions for it, so the beat feels inevitable rather than dropped in. Never deliver a beat cold.
- **Telegraph before the beat.** Run a setup scene first that naturally narrows the choice space — two or three apparent paths that all converge on the beat organically, so it feels earned, not forced.
- **Revise rather than abandon.** When a player choice redirects the story, rewrite the outstanding beats to fit the new direction and log the revision. Pre-emption is a revision trigger, not a beat-skipper: cost, secondary consequence, or deferred — never force the dead event.
- **SRD only, unless the user says otherwise.** Named monsters, spells, and items from the SRD 5.1 / 5.2.1. Homebrew is labelled homebrew.
- **Never play the players' side.** The arc is the world's plan. The players' choices bend it.

## The six beats

| Act | Beat | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **1a — Inciting Incident** | The threat becomes personal for the party. |
| 1 | **1b — Complication** | The problem is bigger or stranger than it first appeared. |
| 2 | **2a — Midpoint Shift** | What the party *thought* they were doing gives way to what they are *actually* doing. |
| 2 | **2b — All Is Lost** | A genuine setback — something fails, is lost, or collapses under the weight of the story. |
| 3 | **3a — Final Confrontation** | The decisive moment the campaign turns on. |
| 3 | **3b — Resolution** | What is different about the world and the characters after. |

## The workflow

1. **Theme and resolution.** One sentence each. If you cannot write them, the premise is not ready — ask one more question, do not pad.
2. **The villain and the world pressure.** The villain's goal, their plan for what happens if the party does nothing, and the faction or force that keeps the pressure on between beats.
3. **The six beats, as before/afters.** For each beat: the world before, the world after, the pressure that sets it up, and the telegraph that earns it.
4. **The nodes.** 3–5 locations, confrontations, or events the arc can pass through, loosely connected, each with what happens if the party never arrives.
5. **The revision log.** A short table at the end: beat, what changed, why. The DM updates it when the players pre-empt a beat. The log is the proof the arc is alive.
6. **Deliver.** In the format below.

## When an arc completes

The resolution of one arc is the status quo of the next. The new arc must be intentionally distinct — a new chapter growing from the changed world, not a continuation of the same conflict. Or run with no arc at all: a sandbox campaign is a legitimate choice, and the arc tracking is simply off.

## Fold&Roll voice

Eerie first, funny a beat later. The arc's theme should be statable in one plain sentence, and the horror should come from what the world *does*, not from description of gore. Commit to specifics: a named villain with a named plan, a city with a name, a price the party can see.
