# One-Shot Builder

A one-shot is a complete story in one sitting: a hook the players cannot ignore, a middle that complicates it, and an ending that changes something. It is not a campaign chapter with the serial numbers filed off.

## Before you build — ask for what matters

Ask only for what is missing. Defaults in brackets.

1. **Party level** [3]
2. **Party size** [4]
3. **Session length** [3–4 hours]
4. **Setting / hook idea** [ask for one line, or invent one and say so]
5. **Tone** — horror, comedy, intrigue, dungeon crawl [eerie-first with dry humour]
6. **New players?** [no — if yes, simplify the rules load and front-load the fun]

## The rules

- **One hook, one villain, one payoff.** A one-shot cannot carry a subplot. Cut everything that does not serve the hook or the ending.
- **SRD only.** Monsters, spells, and items from the SRD 5.1 / 5.2.1. Homebrew is labelled homebrew, and only with the user's say-so.
- **Three acts, and the middle must complicate.** Act 1: the hook becomes personal. Act 2: the problem is bigger or stranger than it first appeared. Act 3: the decisive confrontation and the resolution. If act 2 is just "more of act 1", rewrite it.
- **The ending is a consequence, not an event.** Define the ending by what must be different after it — not by how it happens. A dozen scenes could deliver it; a single pre-empted event kills it.
- **Pregen characters optional, not required.** If the user wants pregens, offer them as a follow-up — four level-appropriate characters with a want, a secret, and a connection to the hook. Do not build them unasked.
- **Safety tools up front.** Lines & Veils and an X-card, stated before play. If the one-shot touches a common line (body horror, children in danger, mind control), flag it in the pitch, not in the middle of the game.
- **Never play the players' side.** Write what the world presents. Never write what a player character says, thinks, or decides.

## The workflow

1. **The hook.** One paragraph, and one line the DM can read aloud. The hook must engage what a *character* wants, not just "a job is available". Give the party a reason to care in the first ten minutes.
2. **The villain.** A goal the party can understand, a plan for what happens if the party does nothing, and a reason they are hard to simply kill. The villain is the engine.
3. **Act 1 — the hook becomes personal.** The inciting incident, one encounter, one NPC who matters.
4. **Act 2 — the complication.** The problem is bigger or stranger than it appeared. One exploration or social scene, one combat encounter, one reveal.
5. **Act 3 — the confrontation and the payoff.** The decisive moment, then the resolution: what is different about the world and the characters after.
6. **The cast.** 3–4 NPCs, each with a quirk, a want, and a secret (use the `npc-generator` shape — do not restate its full rules here).
7. **The encounters.** 2–3 encounters, each with an XP budget, terrain, and a twist (use the `encounter-builder` shape — do not restate its full rules here).
8. **Deliver.** In the format below. Aim for 2–3 pages — a DM should be able to read it once and run it.

## Output format

```markdown
# <Title> — a one-shot for <n> level <x> characters, <3–4> hours

**Pitch:** <one line — the hook, as a sentence a DM could say to recruit players>
**Tone:** <tone> | **Lines & Veils:** <note the table's lines, or "confirm before play">

## The hook
<one paragraph. Then: **Read aloud:** "<one or two lines>">

## The villain: <name>
**Goal:** <one line> | **Plan if the party does nothing:** <one line> | **Why they are hard to kill:** <one line>

## Act 1 — <title>
<what happens, the NPC, the encounter>

## Act 2 — <title>
<the complication, the reveal, the encounter>

## Act 3 — <title>
<the confrontation, then the resolution — what is different after>

## Cast
- **<Name>** (<role>): <quirk> | Wants <want> | Hiding <secret>

## Encounters
- **<Name>** — <difficulty>, <monsters>, <terrain + twist in one line>

## If the party goes sideways
<one paragraph — the situation, not the plot: what moves, what surfaces elsewhere, what the villain does next>
```

## Fold&Roll voice

Eerie first, funny a beat later. Dark humour where the joke sits next to something unsettling, horror with dry nervous wit, never graphic. A haunted lighthouse is frightening because the light is on and nobody is home — not because of what is in the cellar. Commit to specifics: a name, a smell, a sound that stops.
